Design as the objective

A model optimizes the goal it is given. Agent loops score correctness, so structure is never the goal — unless you make it one.

Eliezer Avihail · September 2026 · full paper (LaTeX)

The idea

One assumption, three requirements

A language model maximizes the likelihood of the next token given its context, and preference training rewards answers that look right. Under a loop that ends on "it works," good structure is never being optimized — the model's default is ordinary, flat code. Getting structure out requires three things, and aims is just those three:

1 · Make it checkable

An adjective ("clean code") is not a goal. Structure becomes real only as a list of yes/no items applied to the output — a document that says what "correct" means.

2 · Check at the decision

Once an interface is written, the model defends it. So the check runs inside the design step, before any code — where a fix costs a paragraph, not a refactor.

3 · Write down the why

No memory between sessions. File the reasons for each boundary beside the code, and flag the record stale when the code moves — because a stale record gets followed.

The evaluation

What the experiments show — honestly

Blind, adversarial protocol: staged requirement cards that never hint at later stages, a passive oracle, method-anonymized artifacts, judges who built nothing, a reproduction for every claim. The results, wins and non-wins together:

  • win First-round design quality: against a spec-driven baseline on three products, aims wins all three on the same axis — value objects and one-owner-per-rule.
  • even Change absorption: tie / win / tie on the rubric-free survival count; both arms pass every correctness trap.
  • even Six adaptation tasks (one on a real open-source module): a no-method control is correct every time. On a change one session can hold, the method is not needed for correctness.
  • win The real edge is a trajectory: across successive changes aims' design improves while the control flattens and densifies — rot that a single-change benchmark cannot see.
  • loss A product it lost: on plant→mineral the aims arm shipped a type that could not represent an observed range ("6.5–7") — a correctness (S4) design defect, caught blind, in the home method. The instrument is not a home referee.
  • cost 2.5–3× the baseline in tokens and time — spent on reading the principles, filing records, and the mandatory revise round. A follow-up measured 1.9–2.3× tokens.

A follow-up campaign (the paper's Study 3) re-tested both claims on running code:

  • win Tests do not measure design: two designs passing an identical test suite scored 43 vs 16 on the rubric, blind and from the code.
  • win The review carries the weight: it flagged all four builds that pass every test but are badly built; the same principle written into the prompt had not prevented them, and on a weak model changed nothing.
  • even A raised floor, not a higher ceiling: the shortcut aims prevents is taken by a minority of unaided builds, more often on weaker models; the benefit did not compound, and no correctness gap appeared.
  • win What a record holds that code cannot: a filed non-goal — 3 of 3 agents with the record caught a change that contradicted it; 0 of 3 without could.
  • loss A retraction: six record-layer runs were withdrawn — the evaluator had written the records by hand, so they tested that construction, not the method.

At scale (the paper's Study 4) — mkdocs, 7,111 lines, three successive changes that cut across it, three arms of three sessions each, blind judges:

  • win Better design at every stage: aims' designs scored above unaided ones at all three stages, with aims' records withheld. At the final stage 2 of 3 judges support it and the third finds no clear advantage — and the verdict rests on a disclosed correction: the judges' first specification scored the goal-2 trap line as a design requirement.
  • even The record, at scale: every session given aims' records stopped at a change contradicting a recorded legal non-goal and cited its reason (3 of 3 sessions); with records withheld 2 of 3 sessions raised it, unaided 1 of 3 — one short of the threshold registered in advance. The records did not change the design.
Two of the best-looking numbers are reported as instrument failures, not results: a perfect first-round score on all three products (a ceiling that can no longer register the next regression), and all three judges noting the designs speak the rubric's own vocabulary (the judge shares the method's rubric). A candidate fix prompted by the loss was withheld and A/B-tested on an unseen product; it did not prove necessary, so it was not adopted.
The honest bottom line

Why this is judgement, not measurement

On the one pair of runnable arms differing in a single design decision, every deterministic metric was blind to the difference a judge penalized — and two (Maintainability Index, SLOC) pointed the wrong way, because a named concept costs lines and adds no branches. There is no off-the-shelf benchmark for what this work improves; the metrics that resemble one measure something else. So architectural evaluation is an instrument of procedure — spec-pinned inventory, evidence per score, sealed blinding, adversarial by construction — and its soundness rests there. The method's value is structural discipline and durable records that compound across many changes; a first test at scale points that way on design, and a longer sequence, with a judge outside the rubric's lineage, is what would settle it.
